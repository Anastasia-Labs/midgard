# A8 — Who may submit a block payload to the DA committee?

Recommendation prepared on 2026-10-01. This is a proposed policy, not an implementation or a release-readiness certification. Current working-tree source was inspected at HEAD `3027be19cb3bc740f83af0e155e34ab38c7cf894`; other work is present in this shared checkout. Where the earlier decision brief and current source differ, this document follows the source.

## Recommended decision

Keep public access to retained data and proof material. Restrict the committee's upload endpoint to explicitly authorized producers and committee peers, then require authority for the particular header being uploaded. Producers originate payloads for their own headers. Committee peers may copy those payloads; they must not acquire the power to choose different bytes for somebody else's header.

Preserve a way to recover the exact promised bytes from any honest holder after the original producer goes offline. The preferred recovery mechanism is for the committee to fetch missing data and check it against an existing producer authorization or the frozen availability commitment. This does not require opening the committee's write endpoint to the world.

Once a member signs an availability commitment, its promised bytes must be immutable for that deployment and header. A different upload must not overwrite them or make the original bytes unavailable. Evidence of incompatible authorized promises must remain visible and may refuse further decisions for that header; it must not erase the data needed to respond to an availability challenge.

I agree with narrowing uploads to producer/committee roles as an immediate improvement. I would not describe that role check alone as the complete public-testnet policy. A compromised producer or committee member would still be able to target other producers' headers unless we also bind authorization to the header and bytes.

## What this endpoint does, and why it matters

A payload is the data needed to examine a proposed L2 block and construct a challenge. The header on Cardano records commitments to the block's contents. The DA committee retains the payload and later signs a promise about the exact bytes it can supply. Independent watchers need to retrieve those bytes before the challenge deadline.

That availability is part of optimistic safety, not merely a convenience for syncing. Midgard's security specification explicitly requires an independent party to retrieve committed data, reconstruct the rules, detect violations and finish an admissible proof before a header becomes mergeable. It also states that an L1 header does not itself validate its hashed contents. See [protocol security, lines 41–57](../../../technical-spec/C-considerations/3-protocol-security.tex#L41).

Restricting who can write to the committee therefore has a different purpose from restricting who can read. An ordinary watcher needs data and proof access. It does not need permission to replace the committee's retained record. Keeping these permissions separate protects production while allowing independent scrutiny.

## What established systems teach us

Libp2p authenticates a peer identity; the application must decide what that identity may do. Its security guidance specifically recommends associating identities with permissions and enforcing them in protocol handlers. Noise authenticates the transport identity, not ownership of a Cardano block header. This supports checking the authenticated remote identity before accepting a large upload, rather than trusting an identity supplied inside the request. See [libp2p authorization guidance](https://libp2p.io/docs/security-considerations/#identity-and-trust) and [Noise identity authentication specification](https://github.com/libp2p/specs/blob/master/noise/README.md#static-key-authentication).

Arbitrum AnyTrust separates its sequencer upload API from its public, hash-addressed retrieval API. Its documentation describes restricting uploads to the sequencer, while allowing independent public data servers. Members store the data by hash and sign a hash and expiration time. That is a useful precedent for narrow writes and broad reads. It is not a direct prescription for Midgard, which has multiple scheduled operators and different challenge machinery. See [AnyTrust data servers and sequencer interaction](https://docs.arbitrum.io/how-arbitrum-works/inside-arbitrum-nitro#data-availability-servers).

The OP Stack Alt-DA specification separates storage from commitment submission, checks retrieved input against its commitment, and permits any party to supply the committed input when resolving a supported availability challenge. Its specification is explicitly experimental. The useful principle is that possession of the correct committed bytes can enable recovery without giving the holder power to change the commitment. It does not specify the complete upload authorization policy Midgard needs. See [OP Stack Alt-DA specification](https://specs.optimism.io/experimental/alt-da.html).

My design inference from these sources is: protect the ability to originate a promise, while making the already-promised data easy to copy and retrieve. The distinction matters more than whether the transport is HTTP or libp2p.

## Proposed policy in detail

### 1. Explicit identities and separate permissions

At the private upload boundary, require an authenticated remote PeerID and an explicit producer or committee permission. Reject unknown identities, bootstrap-only identities, and identities holding only watcher, challenger, retrieval or coordinator permission. A process with several legitimate functions can carry several permissions, but a read role must never imply a write role.

Perform this check before reading the full payload frame, waiting for the expensive admission slot or decompressing data. The transport already supplies the authenticated remote identity to request handlers: [dispatch context](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/da/libp2p/DaLibp2pNode.ts#L171). Missing identity must refuse a production upload; an in-process call must not create an accidental bypass.

There is a configuration trap here. The manifest identifies a producer separately in `runtime_topology.producer_peer_id`, and generated committee manifests put that producer address in the bootstrap list. The registry's explicit roles come from committee-member entries. A separate producer can therefore become a known bootstrap peer with no roles. See [manifest bootstrap generation](../../../demo/midgard-node/src/da/libp2p-runtime-manifest.ts#L162), [topology fields](../../../demo/midgard-core/src/da-transport.da-libp2p-runtime-manifest.ts#L87), and [committee config conversion](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/config.libp2p-da-transport-config.ts#L40).

Do not implement this as a bare `roles.includes("producer")` filter and discover after launch that the legitimate producer is blocked. Represent producers explicitly in the authorization registry, independent of committee membership. For an interim single-producer manifest, the declared topology producer may receive an explicit producer grant; other bootstrap entries must receive none. Public multi-operator deployment needs a producer list rather than one global producer identity. Production authority should not require an operator to become a DA committee signer.

### 2. Authority for the particular header

The L1 header already identifies its operator through `operatorVkey`, whose schema is a public-key hash, despite the field name. See [header schema](../../../demo/midgard-sdk/src/ledger-state.header-schema.ts#L20). The libp2p identity is a separate key. The inspected registry stores committee signing keys but no mapping to that operator key hash.

Add an explicit, authenticated binding from each operator's L1 identity to its permitted DA publication identity. A signed delegation by the operator is a strong design: it allows an online network key to publish without using the spending key for every upload. A deployment-controlled mapping is a smaller public-testnet alternative, but it introduces a trusted administrator who must keep that mapping correct through operator joins and rotations. That trust must be stated; a producer role label is not a substitute for it.

For a known L1 header, check the origin against the operator recorded in that header. Use the producer of that historical header, not whichever operator is scheduled now. An operator must be able to repair its old payload after its shift ends. Scheduling and membership checks still determine whether a new block can land on L1; data upload must not invent an additional right to commit blocks.

Bind the producer's authorization to the deployment, header hash, exact envelope digest, schema and byte length, with a distinct signing domain. Include delegation validity and identity where needed. Keep the signature with the data so an honest relay can carry it. This is a proposed wire extension: today's submit request has no producer authorization signature, see [request type](../../../demo/midgard-core/src/da-transport.da-libp2p-runtime-manifest.ts#L256). The existing signed payload announcement authenticates a network-key claim, but still needs a checked relationship to the header's operator: [announcement signing](../../../demo/midgard-node/src/da/libp2p-producer.create-da-libp2p-producer-transport.ts#L90).

If bytes arrive before the receiving committee observes the L1 header, retain only a bounded provisional candidate backed by producer authorization. Do not sign it or make it authoritative until the canonical header is available and the existing verifier succeeds. A temporarily lagging L1 observer should produce a retryable state, not a permanent rejection or weakened check.

### 3. Committee relay and honest recovery

A committee member may resend the original authorized envelope, or bytes that match an independently verified availability commitment already applied on L1. It may not replace the producer's choice with a new choice under its own committee role. The receiver must recheck the authorization and bytes; trusting a relay's word recreates the same problem with fewer attackers.

For recovery after the producer disappears, a committee member should fetch from multiple retained-data sources. Public holders can serve the exact bytes without receiving permission to call the committee's write endpoint. Existing code already fetches from retrieval, bootstrap and committee peers: [payload source selection](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/da/libp2p/payload-source.da-libp2p-payload-source.ts#L36). Extending bounded recovery to public retained-data sources is a natural continuation, not a reason to grant every reader write authority.

If a receiving node has a durable signed or L1-applied commitment, recover precisely the bytes satisfying it. If it has no such commitment, it must use the producer-authorized candidate and run header verification before making a new promise. A commitment copied from a peer is not automatically an L1-applied or locally frozen commitment; its signatures and chain provenance must be verified.

This policy also needs to cover fetched candidates. The current selector marks differing fetched hashes conflicted before it establishes their inner relationship to the header: [candidate selection](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/committee-service.committee-service.ts#L1326). Closing only the upload route would leave an analogous poisoning route through retrieval. This overlaps D1 and should share its storage/conflict design rather than receive an independent exception.

### 4. Immutable promises, separate candidate storage and separate evidence

Use a small conceptual separation: candidate bytes, an accepted binding, and conflicting evidence. Candidate bytes are indexed by their digest. The accepted binding points from deployment and header to the exact promised digest and availability commitment. Conflicting evidence records why another authorized claim disagrees. Receiving bytes must not implicitly rewrite all three.

Do not freeze an arbitrary first upload merely because it was first. A wrong first candidate would otherwise become a permanent denial of service. Before signing, promotion requires checked producer authority, the surviving L1 header, and the existing strict verifier. Truly incompatible authenticated origin claims should conservatively hold new decisions for that header while the conflict is resolved; do not silently choose whichever arrived first.

After signing, do not overwrite or recompress the promised envelope. The inner payload can be identical while identity and zstd envelopes have different hashes. Midgard's availability commitment is derived from exact envelope bytes, and the response code checks those bytes against the frozen commitment: [commitment derivation](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/peer/signatures.ts#L84), [response verification](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/availability/retained-payload.ts#L35).

An exact duplicate is idempotent. Unauthenticated or unrelated different bytes are rejected without changing the retained binding. A genuinely conflicting authorized claim is preserved as evidence and may block new signatures or applies as required by protocol safety, while the original promised bytes remain available to answer the original challenge. Conflicting bytes alone are not automatically an admissible proof of producer fraud or committee double-signing; preserve the signed claims needed to establish the actual offense.

Persist this decision atomically in both supported stores. Recovery must preserve any promise that can still have consequences on the surviving or recoverable L1 history. A rollback, process restart or transport-key rotation must not silently clear the no-double-sign rule. Retention of bytes, bindings, signatures and conflict evidence must follow the shared challenge/recovery horizon, with live obligations pinned.

### 5. Bounded resource use and useful refusals

Authorization reduces the attacker population; it does not make authorized peers harmless. Apply limits on bytes, uploads per peer, provisional candidates per header, provisional storage per producer and across the process, and queued work. Reserve capacity so one faulty producer cannot consume every slot needed by honest producers or recovery. Use expiry for candidates whose headers never land, derived from publication/recovery timing rather than an arbitrary unlimited retention promise.

The current process-wide limiter allows one active upload and uses abortable waiters. It bounds concurrent processing, but its waiter array has no explicit count ceiling. See [admission limiter](../../../demo/da-committee-node/src/da/libp2p/payload-source.da-libp2p-payload-source.ts#L249). Keep its useful limits and deadline; measure before increasing concurrency. Authentication, fair capacity and bounded queues address different problems. Libp2p likewise recommends resource limits and connection gating as complementary controls: [DoS mitigation guidance](https://libp2p.io/docs/dos-mitigation/).

Record the actual remote identity, origin identity, header, digest, authorization outcome and bounded refusal reason. Distinguish unauthorized writes, invalid bytes, temporarily unknown headers, overloaded admission and authenticated conflicting claims. Temporary overload or L1 lag gets a bounded retry hint; authorization failure gets a stable refusal. Malicious requests should not crash the process or permanently disable unrelated headers.

## Alternatives and tradeoffs

| Policy | Benefit | Cost or failure mode | Judgment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Any registered peer may upload | Minimal configuration; easy copying | A read-only or bootstrap peer can pollute records; registry membership becomes excessive authority | Reject for public testnet |
| Producer/committee roles only | Small change using existing identity context; removes unnecessary writers | Any compromised member can still attack another producer's header; may accidentally exclude a bootstrap-only legitimate producer | Useful first step, incomplete alone |
| Original producer only | Clear responsibility; smaller attack surface | Recovery depends on the original producer remaining reachable; historic shifts and key rotation are easy to mishandle | Too restrictive without a relay/recovery route |
| Any public peer may write when bytes match a trusted commitment | Strong recovery after a commitment exists | Public writes still cost bandwidth/CPU; pre-attestation uploads have no independently frozen exact-byte commitment; requires separate abuse controls | Possible later repair API, unnecessary for initial launch if bounded pull exists |
| Explicit producer origin plus constrained committee relay and bounded pull | Prevents unrelated peers choosing records, preserves independent reads and producer-independent recovery | Requires producer identity binding, durable authorization, careful storage and rotation handling | Recommended target |

Adding a payload digest to the block header is another architectural option. It could make exact-byte selection public before committee attestation, but the current payload embeds the header and its hash. Naively adding the hash of that same whole envelope to the header would create a circular dependency. A non-circular commitment to a separately encoded body would require coordinated header, payload, proof and validator changes. The present availability commitment already binds exact bytes after attestation. I recommend using producer authorization for the earlier phase rather than making A8 a new consensus-format project.

## Implementation order and proof required before launch

First establish non-destructive persistence and explicit upload permissions together. Verify the legitimate producer identity is represented correctly. Keep the public listener read-only and preserve later strict validation. This immediately reduces exposure but still assumes upload-authorized members will not attack one another; state that temporary limit plainly.

Then add header-specific producer authorization, relay verification, bounded candidate promotion and exact-commitment repair. Treat these as a shared design with D1's conflict policy and A1's retrieval work. I recommend these protections before advertising a public multi-operator network as resistant to malicious admitted participants. No onchain reset is required merely for a role check; a new signed upload format needs coordinated deployment of its producers and receivers.

Acceptance must demonstrate these cases, not just compile the role filter:

1. Every read-only and bootstrap-only role is refused before upload work; an explicitly authorized separate producer succeeds; unknown or missing authenticated identity is refused.
2. Producer A cannot originate data for producer B's canonical header. A committee relay can carry B's original authorization without changing the digest.
3. Wrong first bytes cannot prevent later authorized, verified bytes from becoming usable. Unrelated second bytes cannot destroy a verified or signed record.
4. A producer that goes offline after publishing does not prevent another holder from restoring the exact frozen bytes. Loss of all honest copies remains unavailable and follows the challenge/timeout protocol; it is never treated as success.
5. Concurrent uploads, restarts, rollback, key rotation and identical retries preserve the frozen binding in JSON and PostgreSQL stores. A different compression envelope cannot alter an existing promise.
6. Authenticated incompatible promises remain observable and cannot make a member sign twice; retaining evidence still permits answering the original availability challenge with its original bytes.
7. Flooding by an authorized peer stays within queue, memory, disk and CPU bounds while an honest producer and an independent watcher continue making progress.

These are proposed acceptance requirements. They were not executed in this task.
